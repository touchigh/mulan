One supplier or several for care-home bathrooms?

Updated 04 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · MULAN editorial team

In short

For a care-home or senior-apartment project, one supplier for the whole aging-friendly bathroom usually gives a more consistent result: matching finishes, handrails and seats that fit together, one set of specifications and one contact for spares. Several suppliers can lower unit prices, but the buyer then carries the work of making every part fit.

Falls are one of the biggest risks in later life. The WHO falls fact sheet describes falls as a leading cause of injury death worldwide and notes that adults over 60 suffer the greatest number of fatal falls. In a care home, the bathroom is where wet floors, transfers on and off the toilet and showering all meet, so the way it is specified matters more than in any other room.

Most bathroom content is written for homeowners choosing a style. Buyers furnishing ten, fifty or two hundred bathrooms for older residents face a different question: should every part come from one factory, or should the project mix the best offer for each item?

What changes when you furnish many bathrooms at once?

A single private bathroom can absorb a mismatch. If a grab bar is a slightly different white from the shower seat, nobody minds. Across a whole building, small differences multiply. Residents move between rooms, staff are trained on one layout, and maintenance teams keep spare parts for every model on site.

Three things tend to go wrong when parts come from many sources:

  • Fit. A wall-mounted seat, a split shower and a handrail may each be fine, but their fixing points, heights and reach were never planned together.
  • Function. A toilet lift and the flip-up armrest beside it need clearance for each other. Two suppliers rarely check that for you.
  • Follow-up. When a part fails, the operator has to work out which supplier owns the problem.

A typical project scenario

Picture an operator opening a 60-bed care home. The design brief asks for a seated shower in every room, support beside every toilet, a call button and a basin a wheelchair user can reach. The purchasing team gets five quotations: showers from one factory, rails from another, cabinets from a third, electrical items from two more. The prices look good. Then the installer finds that the seat clashes with the rail position, the cabinet colour does not match, and the call button supplier cannot say where it should sit relative to the shower. Each fix is small, but sixty rooms turn small fixes into weeks.

Neither option is right for every project. A single supplier wins when consistency, speed of installation and simple after-sales matter most, which is the usual case for care operators. Several suppliers make sense when one item needs a specialist product the main supplier does not make, or when the buyer has its own engineering team to check every interface.

What should a single-supplier package include?

An aging-friendly bathroom has more parts than a standard one. Before comparing quotations, list what the room actually needs:

  1. A way to shower seated, such as a seated shower unit or a wall-mounted seat with a shower that reaches a seated user.
  2. Support at the toilet: a fixed or flip-up armrest, and for users with weak knees a powered lift.
  3. Grab bars at the shower entry and along the wall, ideally ones that are easy to find at night.
  4. A basin and cabinet a wheelchair user can roll under, with a mirror they can see from a seated position.
  5. Safety electrics: lighting suited to older eyes, a presence sensor and an emergency call button.
  6. Warmth: a bath heater, because a cold bathroom is uncomfortable and slows bathing.

The ADA guide to toilet rooms and the guide to bathing rooms are useful references for clearances and grab-bar positions, even outside the United States, because they show how each element affects the next.

How do you keep a mixed supply consistent if you must?

If the project does mix suppliers, a few rules reduce the risk:

  • Agree one colour reference for every visible part and ask each supplier for physical samples.
  • Draw one elevation per bathroom type with every fixing point, then send the same drawing to every supplier.
  • Ask each supplier for load ratings, materials and water or power ratings in writing, so you can compare like with like.
  • Name one party, usually the installer, as responsible for checking interfaces before bulk delivery.
  • Build a mock-up room and have staff and, where possible, residents try it before the full order.

What questions should you ask any supplier?

  • Which parts of the bathroom do you make yourselves, and which do you buy in?
  • Can you plan the room from our floor plan, or only supply items?
  • What are the load ratings of seats, rails and lifts?
  • Which products have electrical parts, and what voltage and protection rating do they use?
  • How do you handle spare parts for a project of this size?

Frequently asked questions

Is one supplier always cheaper for a care-home bathroom project?

Not always. Buying each item from the cheapest source can lower unit prices, but the buyer then pays in time and risk: checking that seats, rails and lifts fit together, matching colours, and managing several warranties. For most care operators the total cost is lower when one supplier plans the whole room.

When does it make sense to use several suppliers?

Use several suppliers when one item needs a specialist product your main supplier does not make, or when your own engineering team can check every interface. In that case, send the same bathroom drawing to every supplier and name one party responsible for checking fit before delivery.

What documents should a bathroom supplier provide for a project?

Ask for load ratings for seats, rails and lifts, materials, water flow and pressure for showers, voltage and protection rating for electrical items, and box sizes for shipping. Having one set of documents in one format makes it much easier to compare quotations and plan installation.

How can a care home test a bathroom specification before ordering?

Build one mock-up room with the chosen products installed at the planned heights. Ask care staff to run a normal shower and toilet routine, and, where possible, ask residents to try it. Fix any clashes in the drawing before placing the full order.
