Care home bathroom design requirements: an 8-part spec

Updated 04 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · MULAN editorial team

In short

A care-home bathroom specification should cover eight parts: space for a wheelchair and a carer, a level slip-resistant floor, seated showering, toilet support such as a rise-assist device, grab bars, an accessible vanity, fixed heating and good lighting, and an SOS call system. Test it in one mock-up room before ordering.

The World Health Organization updated its falls fact sheet on 21 October 2026. It estimates about 842,000 deaths from falls worldwide in 2023, with roughly 71% among adults aged 60 and over. One finding matters directly to anyone building or refitting a care home: according to a 2023 Cochrane review cited by WHO, assessing and modifying home fall hazards cut fall rates by 38% in people already at higher risk. Residents of care homes are, almost by definition, in that group. The bathroom, with water on the floor, transfers on and off fixtures and people moving alone at night, is where many of those hazards sit.

What do most care-home bathroom guides miss?

Most published guidance is written either for architects checking minimum dimensions or for families adapting a single home bathroom. Care-home bathrooms sit between the two. They are used by residents with very different abilities, often several times a day, and almost always with a care worker in the room. A layout that meets the building code can still leave a carer bending over a low toilet, reaching across a resident to adjust the shower, or standing in cold spray.

The people who use these rooms are also rarely asked. In January 2026, the KITE research institute in Toronto described a redesigned shower room on a hospital dementia unit, fitted with a heating panel, a towel warmer, a place to sit and change, glare-reducing lighting and slip-resistant flooring. The work is still being evaluated, but the lead clinician's early impression is that showering has become easier for patients and staff. Warmth, seating and calm are not extras. They change whether a resident accepts help with bathing at all.

What does this look like on a real project?

Take an operator converting an older building into a 40-room residence for older adults, with an en-suite bathroom in each room. The architect has produced a layout that passes local accessibility rules. The procurement team now needs to turn that drawing into a buildable list: what goes on each wall, at what height, in what material, from which supplier. If each part is chosen separately, the result is often a room where the grab bars, toilet, shower and vanity were never considered together. The eight-part checklist below is a way to avoid that.

What are the eight parts of a care-home bathroom specification?

PartWhat to specifyQuestion to ask the supplier
1. Layout and spaceTurning space for a wheelchair plus room for one carer beside the resident; door width and swingDo your products fold or flip up to free floor space?
2. Floor and drainageLevel, step-free shower area; slip-resistant surface; floor falls to the drainWhat is the product's footprint on a sloped wet floor?
3. ShoweringA seated shower or fixed fold-down seat; outlets that reach the back and shoulders; carer access from the sideWhat are the maximum load, water pressure range and flow rate?
4. ToiletSeat height suited to residents; a rise-assist device where many residents struggle to standWhat are the lift range, load rating, water protection rating and service life?
5. Grab bars and supportsBars beside the toilet, in the shower and by the basin; flip-up bars where a carer needs side accessWhat are the bar diameter, inner tube material and colour options?
6. Washbasin and vanityKnee space for seated users, a mirror usable seated and standing, lever controlsCan the mirror tilt, and what clearance is under the basin?
7. Warmth and lightingA fixed heater so the room is warm before bathing; even, low-glare lighting; night lightingIs a heater available with the package, and in what voltage?
8. Monitoring and callAn SOS call button within reach from the toilet and shower; presence sensing that respects privacyHow does the alert reach staff, and does it link to the existing call system?

How should each part be written into the spec?

Layout, floor and drainage

Start with the resident and the carer together, not with the fixtures. Mark where the carer stands for each task: toileting, showering and washing at the basin. Then check every product against those positions. Folding seats and flip-up bars earn their place because they give floor space back when they are not in use.

Showering and toileting

A seated shower lets a resident wash sitting down while staying warm, and keeps the carer out of the direct spray. For toilets, a powered rise-assist stand lowers and raises the user, which reduces the lifting a carer does. Ask for the load rating and the moisture protection rating in writing. Our rise-assist guide covers what to check.

Grab bars and the vanity

Use a consistent bar diameter and finish across all rooms so residents learn one grip. In bathrooms used at night, bars that glow after absorbing light help residents find support without switching on bright lights. For basin height and knee space, see our guide to accessible vanities, and for positions, our grab bar guide.

Warmth, light and call systems

A cold bathroom is a reason residents refuse bathing, and in winter the gap between a warm bedroom and a cold bathroom adds heat shock risk. Specify fixed heating, not portable heaters. Put the call button where a resident who has slipped can reach it from the floor, and agree with the operator who answers it.

How do you check the specification before ordering in bulk?

  1. Build one mock-up bathroom with every specified product fitted.
  2. Ask care staff to run through a full routine in it: transfer, toilet, shower, dry and dress.
  3. Record every point where a carer has to stretch, bend or stand in water, and adjust positions.
  4. Freeze the specification and drawings, including colours and finishes, before the main order.
  5. Keep a spare-parts list per product so maintenance staff can replace seat pads, sleeves and sensors.

Frequently asked questions

What are the minimum design requirements for a care home bathroom?

Minimum dimensions and fittings are set by local building and care regulations, which differ by country, so check the code that applies to your project. Beyond the minimum, a workable care-home bathroom needs space for a carer beside the resident, step-free showering, support at the toilet and basin, fixed heating and a call button reachable from the floor.

Why does a care home bathroom need space for a carer?

Many care-home residents receive help with bathing or toileting, so a second person is usually in the room. If the layout only allows for the resident, the carer ends up bending, stretching across the resident or standing in the shower spray. That raises the risk of strain injuries for staff and makes residents less comfortable accepting help.

Should care homes choose a seated shower or a shower chair?

A shower chair is cheap and portable, but the resident still needs a separate showerhead and the carer often has to hold it. A seated shower combines the seat and the water outlets, so the resident can stay warm while seated and the carer has their hands free. The right choice depends on the residents' abilities and the room's plumbing.

How do you test a care home bathroom specification?

Fit one complete mock-up bathroom with every specified product, then ask care staff to perform a full routine: transfer, toileting, showering, drying and dressing. Note each point where staff must stretch, bend or stand in water, adjust positions and products, and freeze the specification, including finishes and colours, before placing the main order.
