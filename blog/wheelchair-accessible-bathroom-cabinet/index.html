Wheelchair-accessible bathroom vanity: what to specify

Updated 04 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · MULAN editorial team

In short

A wheelchair-accessible bathroom vanity needs open knee and toe space under the basin, a basin rim low enough to reach, clear floor space in front, pipes that are covered or insulated, easy taps, and a mirror a seated person can use. A tilting mirror lets the same cabinet serve seated and standing users.

Most bathroom vanities are designed for someone standing. For a wheelchair user, or an older person who washes sitting down, a standard cabinet blocks the knees, puts the tap out of reach and shows only the top of the head in the mirror. The ADA guide to toilet rooms sets out how lavatories, mirrors and clear floor space work together, and it is a practical reference for care projects in any market.

Falls in bathrooms are a known risk for older adults; the WHO falls fact sheet notes that older people suffer the most fatal falls. A basin that can be used seated removes one reason to stand unsupported on a wet floor.

Who is this vanity for?

In a care home or senior apartment, the same basin may serve a resident in a wheelchair, a resident who stands but tires quickly, and staff helping with washing. The vanity has to work for all three. That is why accessible design focuses on space under the basin and on things the user reaches or looks at, rather than on style.

A typical scenario

A senior-apartment developer fits a wall-hung vanity with a deep drawer under the basin. It looks good in the show flat. The first resident who uses a wheelchair cannot get close enough to the basin to wash, the drain pipe is exposed at knee height, and the mirror starts above his eye line. The fix means replacing the cabinet in every accessible unit. Specifying the right vanity from the start avoids this.

What should you specify under the basin?

  • Knee and toe clearance. Leave the space under the basin open so a wheelchair user can roll forward. The ADA Standards set minimum knee clearance at 27 inches (about 685 mm) high, with toe space below it.
  • Clear floor space. Keep a clear area in front of the basin for a forward approach; the ADA guide uses 30 by 48 inches (about 760 by 1220 mm).
  • Protected pipes. Insulate or cover water supply and drain pipes under the basin, so knees cannot touch hot or sharp parts.
  • No fixed storage in the knee zone. Put drawers and doors to the side, not directly under the bowl.

Why does the mirror matter so much?

A fixed mirror set for standing adults is useless to someone seated. A low fixed mirror, on the other hand, is awkward for a standing resident or for staff. A mirror that tilts forward solves both: tilted down for a seated user, upright for someone standing. It also helps residents of very different heights in the same building.

What else belongs at an accessible basin?

  • A grab bar or basin armrest so the user can steady themselves when moving between wheelchair and standing.
  • Lever or sensor taps rather than small knobs.
  • Lighting that does not throw the face into shadow, which matters more for older eyes.
  • Storage within reach from a seat, placed beside rather than under the basin.

For more on the support side, see our guide on grab bars.

How do you buy accessible vanities for a whole project?

  1. Decide how many rooms need full wheelchair access and how many need seated-use features only.
  2. Ask suppliers for drawings that show knee clearance, rim height and mirror range.
  3. Check that the vanity, basin armrest and mirror come from a range that matches the rest of the bathroom.
  4. Install one sample room and test it with a wheelchair before the full order.

Frequently asked questions

How much knee clearance does a wheelchair-accessible vanity need?

The 2010 ADA Standards set minimum knee clearance at 27 inches, about 685 mm, high under the basin, with toe space below it, so a wheelchair user can roll forward. Local codes vary, so check your market, but keeping the space under the bowl open is the key principle everywhere.

How high should a mirror be for wheelchair users?

Under the 2010 ADA Standards, a mirror above a basin should have the bottom of its reflecting surface no more than 40 inches, about 1015 mm, above the floor. A mirror that tilts forward is a practical alternative because it serves both seated and standing users of the same basin.

Can a vanity have storage and still be wheelchair accessible?

Yes, as long as the storage is beside the basin rather than under it. Drawers or doors in the knee zone block a wheelchair. Side units or a tall cabinet next to the basin keep storage within reach from a seat while leaving space under the bowl clear.

Do pipes under an accessible basin need protection?

Yes. Water supply and drain pipes under an accessible basin should be insulated or covered so a seated user's knees cannot touch hot pipes or sharp edges. This is easy to plan at the specification stage and costly to add later.
