OEM bathroom safety products: how private label works

Updated 04 Nov 2026 · 5 min read · MULAN editorial team

In short

With an OEM bathroom safety products manufacturer, you choose existing models, agree what can carry your brand (logo, colour, packaging, manuals), approve samples, then order under your label. You register and own your trademark, while the factory makes and tests the product. Settle MOQ, lead time, artwork ownership and certificates in writing before the first order.

On 1 October 2026 the WHO published the midpoint report on the UN Decade of Healthy Ageing. It notes that people aged 60 and over passed 1 billion in 2020 and are projected to reach 2.1 billion by 2050, and that only 45.4% of countries have legislation guaranteeing access to assistive devices (WHO report). For distributors and brands, that gap between need and supply is where aging-friendly bathroom products are heading, and many want to sell them under their own name.

Why do distributors choose private label for bathroom safety products?

Selling a factory's brand is quick, but the brand belongs to someone else. If the factory later appoints a second distributor in your market, your customers can buy the same product elsewhere. Private label, often called OEM in this trade, lets you keep the customer relationship, build a range under one name and control how the product is presented in your language.

The trade-off is responsibility. Once your logo is on a shower seat or a grab bar, your customers will hold your brand accountable for its safety. That is why OEM in this category needs more care than OEM for towels or soap dishes.

What does a typical OEM project look like?

A home-furnishing importer in Dubai already sells bathroom accessories under its own brand. A care-home group asks it for seated showers and grab bars to match the rest of a refurbishment. The importer does not want to design a product from scratch. It wants proven models with its logo, its colours where possible, Arabic and English manuals, and cartons that match its other lines. Its questions are practical: what can change, how long it takes, who owns the artwork and who supplies the test documents.

Which OEM route fits your business?

Buyers use the word OEM for several different arrangements. Be clear which one you are asking for, because cost, timing and responsibility change with each.

Most first projects should start as private label on existing models. The product has already been made and checked, so you are only changing what the customer sees. Move to custom changes once you know what sells.

How does the private-label process work, step by step?

  1. Choose the range. Pick a small set of existing models that fit your customers. A seated shower plus two or three grab bar types is a sensible first range for care projects.
  2. Send a written brief. List target market, languages, logo files, preferred colours, packaging style and expected annual volume.
  3. Ask what can be customised. Logo position and method, colour of plastic parts, carton print and manual content all depend on the product and the factory's processes. Get the answer per item.
  4. Protect your brand first. Register your trademark in each market before goods ship. Under the WIPO Madrid System you start from a basic mark at your home office, designate the members you want, and each national office then examines the mark within 12 or 18 months (Madrid System).
  5. Approve artwork and samples. Sign off a branded sample of each model, including packaging and manual, and keep one as the reference for inspection.
  6. Agree the documents. Decide which test reports and certificates are needed for your market and whose name they will carry. For Saudi Arabia, for example, product and shipment certificates go through the SABER platform (SABER).
  7. Place the order and inspect. Check the first production batch against the approved sample before it ships.
  8. Plan reorders. Keep artwork files, colour references and packaging specifications in one shared folder so repeat orders match the first.

Who owns what in an OEM agreement?

Ownership causes more disputes than price. Put these points in the contract before the first order:

  • Trademark: registered in your name, in your markets. The factory should not register it.
  • Artwork and packaging files: state that files made for your brand belong to you and will not be used for other customers.
  • Product design: for private label on existing models, the design usually stays with the factory. For custom work, agree in writing who owns new tooling and drawings.
  • Test reports: agree whether reports will be in the factory's name, yours, or both, and who pays for retesting if you change a material or colour.
  • Exclusivity: if you want it, define it by market, product and time, and expect it to be linked to volume.

What should you check on the product before you put your name on it?

For a seated shower, which is plumbed and weight-bearing, check the rated maximum load, working water pressure, flow rate, materials of the frame and seat, and carton dimensions. Compare the pressure range with typical supply pressure in your projects, and the flow rate with any water-saving rules your customers follow. For grab bars, check tube diameter, inner tube material, fixings and the wall types they suit. If you need help comparing a fixed seated shower with a simpler chair, see this comparison.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between OEM and private label for bathroom safety products?

In this trade the terms overlap. Private label usually means the factory's existing model sold with your logo, packaging and manuals. OEM can also cover changes to the product itself, such as dimensions, materials or functions, built to your specification. Private label is faster and cheaper to start; custom OEM needs more time, volume and testing.

Should I register my trademark before placing an OEM order?

Yes. Register the trademark in your own name in each market where you will sell, ideally before goods ship. The WIPO Madrid System lets you build on a basic mark at your home office and designate other member countries in one application, with each national office examining it under its own law.

Who owns the packaging artwork in a private-label agreement?

It should be you, but only if the contract says so. State that logo files, packaging designs and manual content made for your brand belong to you and will not be used for other customers. For custom products, also agree who owns new tooling, moulds and drawings, and what happens to them if the relationship ends.

What should I check on a seated shower before selling it under my brand?

Check the rated maximum load, working water pressure range, flow rate, frame and seat materials, number and adjustability of outlets, and carton size. Test a sample against the published figures, make sure the pressure range suits the buildings you supply, and confirm which test reports exist for your market before your logo goes on it.
