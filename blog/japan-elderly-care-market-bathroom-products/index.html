Japan elderly care market: a guide for bathroom importers

Updated 04 Nov 2026 · 4 min read · MULAN editorial team

In short

Japan's elderly care market for bathroom products is large and safety-driven: 29.3% of the population is 65 or older, and thousands of older people drown in bathtubs each year. Importers sell through care facilities, home-renovation contractors and welfare-equipment operators, so they need Japanese manuals, voltage-ready electrics, compact sizes and clear documentation from suppliers.

Japan has the oldest population of any major economy, and the numbers keep moving. The Cabinet Office's latest annual report on the ageing society puts the population at 123.80 million on 1 October 2024, with 36.24 million people aged 65 and over, or 29.3%. The 75-and-over group, at 20.78 million, is now larger than the 65-74 group, and by 2070 one person in 2.6 is projected to be 65 or older (Cabinet Office). For importers of bathroom products, the shift towards the over-75s matters most, because that is the age when getting in and out of a bath becomes a daily risk.

Why is the bathroom such a focus in Japan?

Bathing culture is the reason. Many older people in Japan soak in a deep, hot bath every day, often in a bathroom that is much colder than the living room in winter. The Consumer Affairs Agency reports that 8,270 people aged 65 and over died from accidental drowning in 2023, and 6,541 of those deaths happened in bathtubs. Its safety advice is to keep the water at 41°C or below and limit soaking to about 10 minutes (CAA column).

Those figures explain why Japanese buyers look closely at seated showering, warm and well-lit bathrooms, handrails at the tub and toilet, and ways to call for help. Products that make bathing shorter, warmer and more supported fit a problem the government is already campaigning on. Our article on winter bathrooms covers the same risks for other markets.

What does a typical Japanese buyer enquiry look like?

A trading company in Tokyo supplies care facilities and a network of home-renovation contractors. Its clients want seated showers for residents who can no longer use a deep tub safely, toilet rise-assist units for people with knee problems, and handrails that can be fitted in small bathrooms. The company is comparing several overseas suppliers. Its checklist is short and strict: Japanese-language documents, products that work on Japanese mains power, dimensions that fit compact bathrooms, consistent quality across repeat orders and fast answers to technical questions.

Who buys aging-friendly bathroom products in Japan?

Japan's long-term care insurance system shapes much of the market. The health ministry's overview lists rental of care equipment, sales of specified equipment and an allowance for home modification among insured services, with users normally paying 10%, 20% or 30% of the cost depending on income (MHLW overview). Local governments give installing handrails and removing steps as examples of eligible home modifications (Inagi City).

Whether a specific product qualifies for an insurance category is decided under Japanese rules, not by the supplier. Your Japanese partners or care managers should confirm eligibility before you build a range around it.

What product requirements should importers plan for?

  • Power supply. Japan's mains supply is 100 V, at 50 Hz in the east and 60 Hz in the west. Electrical products need to work on both, and you should check with a compliance adviser whether Japan's electrical safety rules apply to each item.
  • Size. Many Japanese bathrooms are compact. Ask for exact dimensions, folded sizes and clearances, not only carton sizes.
  • Language. Manuals, labels and installation guides need to be in Japanese, and the technical terms must be correct.
  • Water supply. For plumbed items such as seated showers, compare the working pressure and flow rate with the buildings you supply.
  • Consistency. Japanese buyers expect the tenth order to match the first. Agree reference samples and inspection standards from the start.

How should you compare international suppliers for Japan?

  1. Ask each supplier for full specifications of the exact models: load, materials, dimensions, voltage and frequency, protection rating.
  2. Ask which test reports exist and whether they cover requirements in Japan. Treat missing documents as a cost and time item in your plan.
  3. Order samples and test them in a real Japanese bathroom, not only in a showroom.
  4. Check whether the supplier can provide Japanese manuals or will accept your translated version for printing.
  5. Discuss whether bundling several items from one supplier, such as a shower, handrails and a toilet aid, simplifies installation and after-sales support. Our specification guide shows how care projects group these items.

Frequently asked questions

How large is Japan's older population?

According to the Cabinet Office's annual report on the ageing society, Japan had 36.24 million people aged 65 and over on 1 October 2024, which is 29.3% of a total population of 123.80 million. The 75-and-over group reached 20.78 million and is now larger than the 65-74 group. By 2070, one in 2.6 people is projected to be 65 or older.

Why are bathroom products important for older people in Japan?

Daily hot baths in cold bathrooms are a serious risk. The Consumer Affairs Agency reports 8,270 accidental drowning deaths among people aged 65 and over in 2023, of which 6,541 happened in bathtubs. It advises keeping bath water at 41°C or below and soaking for no more than about 10 minutes, which supports demand for seated showers and handrails.

Does Japan's long-term care insurance cover bathroom products?

The system includes rental of care equipment, sales of specified equipment and an allowance for home modification, and users normally pay 10%, 20% or 30% of the cost. Local governments cite handrail installation and step removal as eligible modifications. Whether a particular product qualifies is decided under Japanese rules, so confirm eligibility with care managers or local partners.

What should a supplier provide for the Japanese market?

Expect to need full specifications, Japanese manuals and labels, electrical products that run on 100 V at both 50 Hz and 60 Hz, dimensions suited to compact bathrooms and test reports relevant to Japan. Ask each supplier which of these it can provide, order samples for testing in real bathrooms and agree reference samples so repeat orders stay consistent.
